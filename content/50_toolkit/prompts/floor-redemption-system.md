# Floor 1 — Post-Transformation System Design

## 1. THE WORLD ITSELF

The soul storm that has circled Floor 1 since Caesar's conquest is dissipating. Over the course of several days, it thins, breaks, and fades. What it reveals is not the void edge of a floor — it is coastline. Water meeting land. Mountains on the horizon, grey-blue in the distance. The boundary of the floor has become geography.

This is not real water in any conventional sense. It is aberrational — a constructed horizon, a fabricated edge. It behaves like water, looks like water, sounds like water. But it does not lead anywhere. The coastline continues indefinitely. The mountains never get closer, no matter how far you travel toward them. They are the world's edge, rendered as landscape so the mind can accept it. The people who live here will eventually stop noticing they never quite reach the horizon. That is the point.

The world itself is a kind of memory made manifest. Not a memory of a specific place, but the shape of a kind of place — the way cobblestones feel after rain, the particular grey of a stone wall in certain light, the smell of a market street in the morning. It looks like the kind of world IO came from. Not a copy, but an echo. The people who live here have never been to that world. They don't know it exists. But the architecture, the light, the way the shadows fall at certain hours — it feels familiar in a way they can't name.

This is the aberration layer. The entire floor exists in a state of gentle unreality — everything is real enough to touch, to live in, to die in, but it has been arranged. The books in the libraries, if you look too closely, have text that blurs when you try to read it directly. The streets, if you trace them on a map, form patterns that shouldn't be possible. Nobody notices this day to day. It only becomes visible when you look for it. Most people never do.

The floor's new name is not yet settled. It will earn one.

> *Visual references: Baldur's Gate city architecture, the Sword Coast coastline, .hack's Mac Anu as interface overlay. Evangelion's geo-front as the boundary — a constructed horizon that serves its purpose perfectly.*


## 2. THE DUNGEON SYSTEM

### The Calendar

The floor operates on a standardized cycle. Days and weeks are marked not by tradition but by the behavior of the dungeons themselves. A calendar has emerged — not imposed, but observed — based on which dungeons are open, which are closed, which are about to shift.

Each dungeon is accessible on a specific cycle:
- **Some are daily** — open every day, reset at midnight, always available.
- **Some are weekly** — open only on certain days of the week. A dungeon that opens on Windsday stays closed the other six days.
- **Some are phase-locked** — tied to the state of other dungeons. They open when certain conditions are met elsewhere on the floor.
- **Some are unpredictable** — their schedule is not yet understood. They appear and vanish on rhythms nobody has mapped.

When a dungeon is closed, its entrance is dormant — the portal does not activate, the attraction façade is dark, the entryway leads nowhere. The locals know this. They plan around it.

### The Attractions

Every dungeon has a surface entrance that reads as an attraction. Not garish — medieval-fantasy appropriate. A carved stone archway inscribed with symbols. A door in a hillside with iron fittings. A well that glows faintly at certain hours. A standing stone circle that hums when it's active.

The entrance is not the dungeon. It is the threshold.

### The Pocket Realm

Stepping through the threshold transports you into a contained demiplane — a pocket realm that exists only while the dungeon is active. Inside, the environment is complete and self-contained:

- A frozen tundra inhabited by crystalline elves that attack on sight.
- A forest of silver trees where the leaves are razor-sharp and the paths rearrange themselves.
- A sunken chapel at the bottom of an underground sea, its bells still ringing.
- A castle of glass where every reflection is a potential enemy.

The pocket realm has a clear objective, which is always communicated through the interface: *defeat all hostile entities, reach the heart of the realm, survive until the crystallization.*

When the objective is achieved, the pocket realm destabilizes. The environment begins to collapse — the sky cracks, the ground fissures, the edges crumble into light. A treasure room materializes in the final moments, accessible briefly before the collapse completes. Delvers must reach it and exit before the realm fully closes.

The exit deposits them back at the entrance hex. Behind them, the pocket realm is gone. The entrance may remain active (for a daily dungeon) or go dormant (for a weekly or phase-locked one).

### The Replacement Cycle

When a dungeon is completed — not just cleared once, but fully resolved — it collapses in a different sense. The pocket realm doesn't just close. It merges with what remains of other completed dungeons. Two, three, a constellation of them, folding into each other.

From that collapse, a new dungeon spawns. Its location on the hex map may be different. Its theme may be different. Its difficulty, its rewards, its schedule — all may shift. The new dungeon replaces what was lost. Something ends. Something begins.

This is the economy. Not soul stones, not trade goods — rotation. The floor is a living catalogue of experiences that arrive, are consumed, and are replaced by something new.

### Rewards

On completing a dungeon, the delver receives:

1. **Soul stones** — base reward, scaled to difficulty. The floor's underlying economy still uses them.

2. **A random item** — drawn from a pool associated with that dungeon's theme. Frozen elves drop crystalline gear. Glass castles drop reflective items. The pool is replaced when the dungeon cycles.

3. **Rarely, a particular order** — a token that can be redeemed for a specific request. These are the real currency. Accumulate enough, and you can commission something: a custom item, a piece of information, an audience with someone important on the floor.

4. **Extremely rarely, the exit token** — an item that grants the bearer passage out of the House entirely. Not to another floor. Out. The offer is presented once. The bearer decides.

### The Gotcha

The rewards are randomized within tiers. You never know exactly what a dungeon will give you until you complete it. The same dungeon, run by different groups on different days, produces different results. The system is generous enough that you always get something. It is calibrated enough that the something you want is rarely the something you get.

This is intentional. It keeps people coming back. It keeps the economy flowing. It keeps the floor alive.


## 3. IO'S ROLE

IO does not run the dungeons directly. The dungeons run themselves — they are the floor's expression of freedom, manifesting and collapsing on their own logic.

But IO decides when a cycle completes. When enough dungeons have been cleared, when enough pocket realms have collapsed, a threshold is reached. At that point, IO can:

- **Choose what spawns from the collapse.** The new dungeon's broad direction — not its exact content, but its theme, its difficulty tier, its place in the calendar.
- **Authorize the next gotcha pool.** What items, what orders, what probabilities are in the random draw.
- **Hold the exit token.** It only drops when IO allows it. The token exists in the system, but it won't manifest until IO opens that door.

IO's role is subtle. The people who live here and the delvers who visit experience the system as autonomous. They don't see the gatekeeper. They just notice that sometimes, right after a major collapse, the quality of rewards shifts. The new dungeon that appears feels different from the old one. The calendar seems to adjust itself.

IO is the wind. You feel the change, but you don't see what caused it.
